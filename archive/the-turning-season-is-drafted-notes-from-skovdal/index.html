The Turning Season Is Drafted: Notes from Skovdal

Book five of The Monstrous Beloved is drafted, and its valley has become one of the series' most intimate haunted places.

By Selena Monroe · July 30, 2026 · 6 minute read

The Turning Season is drafted. That sentence looks small on the page, which is rude of it, frankly, because this book was not small while I was inside it. Book five of The Monstrous Beloved took the series out of ruined estates, coastal stations, old cities, and impossible basements, and brought it into a valley that knows exactly who has left, who has stayed, and what everyone has learned not to say out loud.

This is a spoiler-light note, so I am not going to talk about the exact shape of the ending. What I can talk about is what the draft taught me about Skovdal, Elowen, Tarn, and the particular kind of haunting that comes from returning to a place that still recognizes you after you have spent years trying not to recognize yourself.

A Valley with a Memory

Skovdal became one of those settings that refuses to behave like background. It is a fictional Scandinavian valley with distinct zones: the settlement on the valley floor, the forest edges where old habits still live, and the moorland above, exposed and borderlike. Each space asks something different of the characters. Each one has a different relationship to safety.

That mattered because The Turning Season is a homecoming story, and homecoming is rarely gentle in gothic romance. Home is not only comfort. It is witness. It remembers who you were before the damage, which can be tender, but also unbearable. Elowen returns to Skovdal carrying shame she has mistaken for truth. The valley does not immediately fix that. It simply refuses to collude with the version of herself she learned elsewhere.

The Body Restored to Itself

Every Monstrous Beloved book has its own monster logic. For The Turning Season, the central bodily question is not predator and prey. It is restoration. Shapeshifting here is not written as a curse to escape or a horror to overcome. It is physical truth. It can be frightening because other people have made it dangerous, but the body itself is not the enemy.

That distinction shaped the whole draft. Elowen's suppression reflex is not only magical control. It is the habit of making herself smaller before anyone can punish her for taking up space. Tarn carries a different version of the same pressure: not flight, but endurance. She stayed. She managed. She learned the shape of every boundary around the community, and that kind of survival has a cost too.

Writing them meant paying attention to the difference between shame and injury. Shame says, "This is what I am." Injury says, "This is what happened to me." The book lives in the long, difficult movement between those two sentences.

Why the Registry Matters Here

The Registry thread in The Turning Season is quieter than in some of the other books, but quiet does not mean absent. By book five, the Registry is not simply an institution that arrives at the door. It is the pressure already built into the landscape: watch classifications, old files, rules that shape what a community is willing to risk, and the kind of bureaucratic harm that can become normal if it lasts long enough.

I liked writing that kind of institutional pressure because it does not require a villain standing in every scene. The threat is structural. It shows up in business offers that sound reasonable until you see the leverage underneath. It shows up in community exhaustion. It shows up in the difference between being free and being less trapped than you were yesterday.

The Romance of Almost Saying It

Elowen and Tarn knew each other before the damage. That changed the rhythm of the romance. They are not strangers learning the outline of each other for the first time. They are people standing in the gap between what they knew, what they guessed, and what neither of them could bear to name.

A lot of this draft became an exercise in restraint: conversations that almost happen, sentences that turn away at the last moment, old intimacy that has not disappeared but cannot be trusted yet. There is a particular ache in writing two people who still understand each other's silences and still manage to misunderstand what those silences mean.

That is the engine of the book for me. Not whether love exists. It does. The question is whether love can survive the truth arriving late, and whether two people can stop treating shame as evidence.

What Comes Next

A completed draft is not the same thing as a finished book. The Turning Season still has revision, proofing, formatting, and the usual final passes ahead of it. But the shape is there now. The valley is built. Elowen and Tarn have walked the road in and the road through. The Registry has left its mark, and the series has moved one step closer to the record it has been building from the beginning.

The Turning Season releases September 9, 2026. If you have been following The Monstrous Beloved from the beginning, this is the book where the series turns toward home and asks what it costs to stay.
