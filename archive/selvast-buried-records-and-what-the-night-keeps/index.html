Selvast, Buried Records, and What the Night Keeps

The final book of The Monstrous Beloved brings a journalist and a vampire into a city where surviving history and official history are rarely the same thing.

By Selena Monroe · October 6, 2026 · 5 minute read

What the Night Keeps arrives on October 7, bringing The Monstrous Beloved to its sixth and final romance. Its city is Selvast: old stone, inconvenient records, and institutions that have survived several changes of name. If you have followed the Registry through the earlier books, this is where the paper trail becomes somebody's daily life.

This entry stays with the setting and the questions the book opens. The investigation's discoveries, the sealed files, and the ending belong to the novel.

A City Built Over Itself

Selvast sits somewhere between the Balkans and the Caucasus, in a deliberately unspecified country. Its Byzantine foundations carry Ottoman additions and later Central European layers. Those layers remain in use. A building can have served several vanished administrations and still need its stairs repaired.

That practical age matters. The city has been contested, renamed, and administered by powers that no longer exist. The Registry has persisted through that history. So has Lucrezia, who has lived long enough to remember what an official account leaves out.

In a sapphic vampire romance, centuries can easily become shorthand for glamour or loneliness. Selvast gives those centuries an address. Time has left changes of ownership, gaps in municipal records, and streets where the newer city stops abruptly enough for someone to notice she has crossed into a different kind of knowledge.

The Kadran and Novibrod

The Kadran is the old quarter: narrow streets, worn stone, and buildings adapted repeatedly rather than preserved as exhibits. It is Lucrezia's territory. Late-autumn light reaches it briefly, then the shadows settle quickly. The darkness there is ordinary enough to live in.

Novibrod belongs to the contemporary city. It has municipal archives, construction, cafes, and Maren's hotel. Glass and steel stand over older foundations; paid-for signs share space with signs nobody has bothered to remove. The argument between eras continues at street level.

Maren is an investigative journalist. Novibrod gives her the working surfaces she recognizes: a desk, a reading room, documents she can request. She stays in a hotel because she has arrived to do a job. Her suitcase remains staged for departure. Walking down toward the Kadran takes her into a place that cannot be approached entirely through the routines she brought with her.

Halvet House and the Record That Resists a Catalogue

Halvet House is old, but it is not supernatural. It does not remember for Lucrezia or decide who may enter. Its history exists in the building's use and in the records she keeps inside it.

Those records have no obvious system a stranger could follow. Their security depends on knowing how Lucrezia thinks. Maren can read a page without knowing where it belongs, or what another page would change about it. The work requires access to the person who has kept the material, as well as access to the material itself.

That is fertile ground for intimacy. A question about a document becomes a question about what someone has chosen to keep. An answer may require explaining a decision made long before the other woman was born. Trust grows through what each is willing to disclose, and what each does with the disclosure afterward.

The Reading Room Has a Closing Time

The municipal reading room has strip lighting, hard chairs, and bad coffee in decent cups. Its holdings have practical limits. Some older material is kept elsewhere, with different opening hours; some records have been lost. Maren has to work around those conditions before she can even begin to weigh what a document means.

The small inconveniences give the investigation its texture. A missing record can be an accident. A restricted opening day can simply be an opening day. Yet each affects whose history can be recovered, by whom, and how much time the search takes.

At Sedlar House, the Registry's station, that procedural texture becomes more troubling. Unremarkable rooms and routine paperwork can carry decisions with consequences far beyond the people processing them. The Registry's confidence travels easily through a form, even when the knowledge behind it is incomplete.

What a Journalist and a Vampire Owe the Record

Maren needs evidence she can verify and publish. Lucrezia has reasons to treat disclosure as dangerous. Both positions come from experience, and bringing them into the same room does not make their obligations disappear.

The romance develops inside that difficulty. Being trusted with someone's history asks more than curiosity. It asks what you will make public, what you will protect, and whether the person who gave you access still gets a say.

After five books of creatures and women encountering the Registry's categories, Selvast brings the series close to the people who preserve those encounters. Someone has to keep the record. Someone has to read it. And between those two acts, there is room for desire, disagreement, and the slow work of becoming trustworthy.
